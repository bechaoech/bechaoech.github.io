Our Green Card Journey

When we first arrived in the U.S., neither of us imagined that we would be starting a green card journey only about a year later. Back then, we thought this was something that would come much further down the road — perhaps after my husband finished his PhD and had the degree we believed he would need before even being able to apply. ✨

But life has a funny way of taking us somewhere we never expected. One thing slowly led to another, and before we knew it, a possibility that once felt so far away had become something we were seriously preparing for.🌱

This page is where I want to keep that story — from the first time we seriously began thinking about a green card, through the preparation of our EB-2 NIW petition, and eventually to the adjustment of status process when that chapter begins.

Current chapter I-140 filed with Premium Processing 📄

Background

Before coming to the U.S., neither of us had ever considered becoming permanent residents here. Our plan at the time was quite simple: my husband would pursue his PhD in the U.S., we would spend those years learning, growing, and building our experience, and eventually return to Vietnam to continue our careers there.

But after living in the U.S. for around six months, we slowly began to see things from a different perspective. As international students, so much of our daily life and our future plans were tied to our immigration status. There were limits on where and how we could work, many decisions required us to think carefully about maintaining status, and even traveling outside the U.S. meant preparing documents, thinking about re-entry, and accepting a certain amount of uncertainty every time we left the country.

At the same time, the immigration landscape around us seemed to be changing constantly. We often found ourselves reading new announcements, trying to understand updated rules, and wondering how future policy changes might affect international students like us. For the first time, we started thinking about how different life might feel if we no longer had to plan every major decision around a temporary immigration status.

That was when the idea of becoming permanent residents first entered our minds. It was not something we had planned before coming to the U.S., and at that point we still had no idea whether it would even be possible for us. But if there was a path that could allow us to build our lives here with a little more stability and freedom, we felt that it was worth learning about.

Our Full Green Card Journey

Just like many of the chapters that came before this one, our green card journey did not begin with a plan. We had no roadmap or timeline — somehow, everything unfolded naturally. One small thought led to a conversation, then to a little more research, and step by step, we found ourselves moving toward a decision we had never expected to make so soon.

I want to document this journey so that one day, we can look back and remember all the little decisions, uncertainties, and unexpected turns we went through together. And if our experience can offer even a little encouragement or guidance to someone who finds themselves on a similar path, that would make this record even more meaningful.

Our journey is divided into two chapters: Part I follows our EB-2 NIW and I-140 journey, while Part II will continue with Adjustment of Status when that chapter begins.

1. When We First Started Thinking About a Green Card

Before coming to the U.S., neither of us really knew what life as an international student would actually feel like. We had both grown up and studied in Vietnam, and only a couple of years had passed since we finished university before we moved abroad for the first time. Until then, neither of us had ever lived outside Vietnam. At that point, immigration status still felt like something written on a visa or a piece of paperwork, rather than something that could quietly shape so many everyday decisions.

Our understanding was very simple. My husband would come to the U.S. on an F-1 visa, complete his PhD, and hopefully stay for a few more years to gain research and work experience afterward. We knew that F-1 students could potentially work after graduation through OPT, and that students with qualifying STEM degrees might be able to extend that period further. To us, that already sounded like more than enough time. As for a green card, the only path we really knew about back then was family sponsorship. Since neither of us had close relatives in the U.S. who could sponsor us, permanent residency never felt like something that was even within reach.

It was only after we actually started living here that we began to understand how different life could feel when almost every important decision was tied to a temporary immigration status. In Vietnam, being Vietnamese citizens meant that we never had to think twice about whether we were allowed to take a job, work additional hours, or change employers. In the U.S., an F-1 student has to follow a much more specific set of rules. During the academic term, qualifying on-campus employment is generally limited to 20 hours per week, while working off campus requires the proper authorization and has to fit the conditions of that authorization. Even something as ordinary as accepting an internship or working additional hours could not simply be decided based on whether there was enough time in the week — we first had to make sure it was permitted under his F-1 status.

That was probably the first time we realized how carefully international students have to understand the rules surrounding their status. Unauthorized employment, or even working beyond what is permitted, can have serious immigration consequences. It made us feel that there was very little room for simply assuming that something was allowed; whenever a new opportunity appeared, our first question often had to be, “Are we actually allowed to do this?”

My own F-2 status came with an even different set of limitations. As an F-2 dependent, I am not authorized to work in the U.S. I can take part-time postsecondary classes, but if I wanted to pursue a full-time academic program, I would generally need to change to a student status of my own. These were rules I already understood before we moved, but experiencing them as part of daily life gave them a very different weight.

Travel was another part of the experience that slowly changed our perspective. For Vietnamese students like us, F-1 and F-2 visas are currently issued with a validity period of up to 12 months. We later learned that an expired visa does not mean we have to leave the U.S. as long as we continue maintaining our lawful status here. But once we travel abroad, things become more complicated: if the visa stamp has expired, returning to the U.S. will generally require obtaining a new visa before re-entry, subject to limited exceptions.

That made even ordinary travel decisions feel heavier than we had expected. A conference outside the U.S., a visit back to Vietnam, or simply wanting to see family could mean thinking about visa validity, a new visa application, re-entry, and the possibility that the process might not go exactly as planned. And with Vietnam being more than a day of travel away from the U.S. for many itineraries, none of those decisions felt particularly small.

None of these things made us suddenly decide that we needed a green card. There was no single moment when everything changed. Instead, little experiences like these slowly accumulated. We began to understand why having permanent resident status could bring a kind of stability and freedom that we had never thought much about before — more flexibility in building a career, fewer immigration-related restrictions in everyday life, and less uncertainty around long-term plans and travel.

And somewhere along the way, a question that had never crossed our minds before quietly appeared:

“What if becoming permanent residents was actually possible for us?”

That was the first time the idea of a green card truly entered our minds.

2. Learning About Our Options

When we first arrived in the U.S., a lot of what we knew about green cards came from conversations with older students and friends who had already been here for a while. Since my husband was also pursuing a PhD, we naturally paid attention to people who had taken a similar path before us.

From those conversations, we gradually picked up a very general picture. For PhD students, one common route we heard about was to first earn a master’s degree along the way, if the program allowed it, and then start the green card process from there. Otherwise, some people simply waited until they completed their PhD before beginning. We also noticed that many of them chose to work with an immigration attorney rather than prepare everything on their own.

At that stage, though, our understanding was still very superficial. We knew that there were ways for researchers and PhD students to pursue permanent residency, but we had no clear idea what immigration category they were actually using, what qualifications were required, or what the process really looked like in practice.

3. Why We Chose EB-2 NIW

After spending some time learning about the different employment-based green card categories, EB-2 NIW gradually stood out to us as the path that seemed to fit our situation best. It was the first option that made us feel that permanent residency might actually be something we could realistically explore.

From there, I started reading much more about EB-2 NIW through places where people often shared their own experiences — Facebook groups, Reddit discussions, and other online communities. I came across stories from researchers, PhD students, and people with backgrounds that felt somewhat similar to ours, and little by little, I began comparing their situations with my husband's profile.

At that point, our understanding was still far from complete. We did not yet fully understand how USCIS evaluated an NIW petition or what made a case truly strong. But based on what we had learned from other people's experiences, it started to seem that his background might be enough to at least seriously consider filing an EB-2 NIW petition. That was when EB-2 NIW went from being just another immigration category we had read about to becoming the path we wanted to explore more carefully.

4. Evaluating Our Case

As I continued reading through Facebook groups, Reddit discussions, and information from USCIS, I started to form a rough picture of what people usually looked at when evaluating an EB-2 NIW case for someone in computer science research. In the communities I followed, the same things came up again and again: publications at reputable conferences or journals, citation counts, peer-review experience, and evidence that other people in the field recognized or relied on the applicant's work.

I also learned that support letters were not strictly required, but they could help strengthen the story behind a petition — especially when they came from established researchers or other people who could independently speak about the significance and impact of the work. From what I read, letters from someone connected to a U.S. government agency, or from an organization with a genuine interest in the proposed work, could be particularly helpful because they might provide independent evidence that the work had value beyond one research group or employer and could support the argument that the proposed endeavor was important to the United States on a broader, national level.

So I started looking at my husband's profile through that very simple lens. At the time, he had 12 research works in total: 2 peer-reviewed journal articles, 9 peer-reviewed conference papers, and 1 technical report. His work had received around 176 citations, and he had also completed 11 peer reviews for academic conferences and journals.

Of course, I still did not understand all the legal details behind how USCIS actually evaluated an NIW petition. I was mostly comparing his profile with the cases and discussions I could find online. But based on that very early self-evaluation, I felt that his research record looked reasonably strong and that filing an EB-2 NIW petition might actually be realistic for us.

That was the first point when the idea began to feel less like a distant possibility and more like something we could seriously consider.

5. Deciding Whether to File Ourselves or Get Help

After reading more personal experiences and discussions on Facebook groups and Reddit, I learned that an EB-2 NIW petition could be self-filed. For a while, I seriously considered whether we should try to prepare everything ourselves.

But the more I read, the more I realized how different it was to understand our own research achievements from knowing how to present them properly in an immigration petition. I could understand, in a general sense, why my husband's research might be valuable and how his work could contribute to the United States. What I did not understand nearly as well was the legal side of the process — how the relevant immigration rules were interpreted, how different legal standards connected to one another, or how to explain our evidence using the language and structure expected in a petition.

Documents such as the petition letter and the proposed endeavor were especially intimidating to me at the time. I knew the facts of our own case, but turning those facts into a clear and well-supported argument was something completely different. I did not feel confident that I could identify the right legal points, organize all of the evidence properly, and explain why each piece actually mattered to the case.

So although self-filing was technically an option, our first instinct was to lean toward working with an immigration attorney. We felt that if we were going to take this step, we would rather have someone with experience help us understand the process and present the case as carefully as possible.

6. Choosing Our Immigration Attorney

Once we decided that we would probably feel more comfortable working with an attorney, the next question was which firm to choose. From what I found in research communities, especially among people in computer science and other academic fields, the two names I came across most often for cases like ours were Ellis Porter and Chen Immigration. There were certainly other firms, but at the time, these were the two I saw discussed most frequently.

Before making any decision, I spent quite a bit of time reading other people's experiences with both firms. Around that time, I came across a number of comments from people who said they had been disappointed with the recent quality of work they received from Ellis Porter — for example, having to carefully review drafts themselves, point out mistakes, or request substantial revisions. Some also connected their RFEs with issues they felt should have been handled more carefully. Of course, these were individual experiences shared online, and I had no way of knowing whether they represented the firm as a whole, but seeing the same concerns come up repeatedly made me a little hesitant.

The feedback I found about Chen Immigration was generally more positive, but I still did not want to make a decision based only on online opinions. Since both firms offered free case evaluations, I decided to submit my husband's profile to both and see how they would evaluate the same case.

Ellis Porter told us that because my husband did not yet have a master's degree, they were not willing to take the case at that time and suggested that we contact them again after he obtained one. Chen, on the other hand, looked at the case from a different angle. They asked whether we would be comfortable filing under the Exceptional Ability route and explained that, if my husband joined IEEE as a professional member, they believed they could move forward with the petition on that basis.

Chen also offered us what they called an Approval or Refiling arrangement, together with several preparation options depending on how much supporting documentation we wanted to include. The options ranged from an accelerated service without support letters, to packages that included two or four support letters and a more comprehensive level of preparation.

At that point, the difference between the two evaluations made our decision much easier. One firm essentially asked us to wait until my husband had another degree, while the other saw a possible path using the profile he already had and was willing to explain how they would build the case around it.

There was also one more factor that made timing feel especially important to us. Around that time, we learned that the EB-2 category for Vietnam had only recently become current again after being backlogged for more than three years. Seeing that made us realize that visa availability was not something we could simply assume would always stay the same.

Because of that, we did not really want to wait another year or two for my husband to obtain a master's degree if there was already a reasonable way to move forward with the profile he had. Filing earlier would at least allow us to establish our priority date sooner and, if the category remained current and we later became eligible to adjust status, potentially continue to the next stage without adding unnecessary waiting time.

That made Chen's Exceptional Ability approach much more appealing to us. Instead of asking us to wait for another degree, they were willing to evaluate the case based on the research record my husband already had and suggested building the EB-2 petition through the Exceptional Ability route, with IEEE membership as part of that strategy.

At that point, the decision became much clearer. We already wanted professional help, Chen was willing to take the case as it stood, and the Visa Bulletin gave us another reason not to delay if we did not have to. So rather than waiting for a future master's degree, we decided to move forward with Chen and pursue EB-2 NIW through Exceptional Ability.

7. Getting Started with Chen

Once we officially decided to move forward with Chen and signed the agreement, we were given access to their online case-management portal. From that point on, almost everything related to our petition happened there — instructions, questionnaires, document uploads, messages with the legal team, drafts, revisions, and eventually the final filing package.

At first, the portal looked a little overwhelming because there were quite a few sections to go through. But Chen did not expect us to figure everything out on our own. They gave us a sequence for getting started, and once we followed it one step at a time, the process became much easier to understand.

One small thing that was surprisingly important was learning how to communicate through the portal itself. Whenever we uploaded something for Chen to review, we were supposed to send a separate message through the Messages section so that the legal team would actually be notified. Simply leaving a note with the uploaded document was not enough.

Step 1: Reading the Client Packet

Before filling out the case materials, Chen asked us to first go through the Client Packet available under the Overview page. They specifically encouraged clients to read all of the materials there, especially the Frequently Asked Questions for Current Clients, because many common questions about the preparation process were already addressed in those documents.

The Client Packet was basically our reference library for the case. Instead of being one document that we had to memorize from beginning to end, it contained different guides that became useful at different stages of the petition. There were materials explaining things such as the proposed endeavor, support letters, evidence preparation, citation records, and other parts of the NIW process.

Chen also provided a separate Visa FAQ section with information about topics that could affect the green card process, including travel, Adjustment of Status versus immigrant visa processing, priority dates, change or extension of status, job changes, marriage, and address changes.

We definitely did not remember everything after reading it once. What helped us more was simply understanding where the information was located. Whenever a new question came up later, we usually knew which guide or FAQ to return to.

Our takeaway: Read through the Client Packet once before seriously filling out the case. You do not need to memorize every page, but knowing what resources are available makes the rest of the process much less confusing.

Step 2: Completing the Client Record

The next important section was the Client Record. I would describe this as the place where Chen collected the core personal and immigration information they needed to understand our situation and later prepare the immigration forms.

It was much more detailed than simply entering a name and address. The Client Record asked us for information such as:

  • The type of petition being prepared and whether we wanted Premium Processing
  • Our tentative plan for the later green card stage, such as Adjustment of Status or immigrant visa processing
  • Legal name and other personal information exactly as shown on official documents
  • Current nonimmigrant status and relevant visa information
  • Passport information
  • Most recent I-94 and U.S. entry information
  • Immigration-history and background questions
  • Current residential address and contact information
  • Marital status and dependent information

For someone already in the United States, there were several immigration details that needed a little more attention. One example was the I-94. Chen asked for information from the most recent I-94, including the Admit Until Date. For someone in F-1 or F-2 status, this will normally appear as D/S, or Duration of Status, rather than an ordinary calendar expiration date.

The biggest thing we paid attention to here was consistency. Whenever something could be checked against an official document, we tried not to rely on memory. Names came from passports, immigration information came from the I-20, visa stamp, and I-94, and dates were checked rather than guessed.

Chen gave us similar advice about the CV. Job titles, degree dates, and other factual details should match the supporting documents because the same information would later appear again in the I-140-related forms. Their team would eventually cross-check the CV against the forms during the Form Stage.

At the same time, we also learned that this did not mean the very first version of the CV had to remain unchanged throughout the entire case. Case preparation can take time, and the strategy can still evolve as the attorneys learn more about the applicant's background, proposed endeavor, and available evidence. New research may be published, citation counts may change, peer-review activity may increase, a position may change, or certain experiences may become more or less relevant to the way the petition is eventually presented.

So for us, the goal was not to make the CV “perfect” on day one. The more important thing was to make sure that whatever information was included was accurate, supported by the underlying documents, and updated whenever something meaningful changed. As the case developed, we revised the CV when necessary so that it continued to reflect the version of my husband's background that the petition was actually being built around.

What we paid attention to: We tried to separate the parts of the case that should remain factual and consistent from the parts that could naturally evolve during preparation. Names, dates, immigration history, degree information, and other objective facts should match the official records. The CV and the way the overall profile was presented, however, could still be updated as the case developed — as long as every update remained accurate and consistent with the supporting evidence.

Step 3: Updating My Profile

The My Profile section served a different purpose. While the Client Record was mostly about personal and immigration information, My Profile gave Chen a quick picture of the academic and research background behind the NIW case.

This section asked for information such as:

  • Degrees already earned
  • Any advanced degree currently in progress
  • Whether those degrees were related to the proposed endeavor
  • A public citation profile, such as Google Scholar
  • Current U.S. position
  • Proposed employment in the United States
  • Whether the proposed work would remain in the same or a similar field
  • Whether research and publication activity were still continuing and aligned with the proposed endeavor
  • Peer-review activity

Chen also noted that the citation profile should be publicly accessible and that the applicant should be correctly listed as an author on the publications shown there.

This part made us think about the case a little differently. Until then, we mostly thought of the profile as a collection of accomplishments — degrees, papers, citations, reviews, and so on. But the questions in My Profile made it clearer that Chen was also trying to understand the continuity of the story.

What had he worked on before? What was he working on now? And did it naturally connect to what he intended to continue doing in the United States?

For our case, we wanted those pieces to make sense together. Before considering this section finished, we compared it with the CV, Google Scholar profile, and the general research direction we expected to use in the petition. We wanted the same person and the same research trajectory to come through everywhere.

Our takeaway: My Profile looks simple, but it is more than a short academic résumé. We saw it as an early snapshot of the story that the rest of the NIW petition would eventually need to support.

Step 4: Preparing the Information That Would Build the Case

Once the basic setup was done, we moved into the parts of the portal that were much more specific to building the NIW petition itself.

The main sections we would work through next were:

  • PE/PE — Proposed Employment / Proposed Endeavor: where we began defining the proposed endeavor, future research direction, and proposed employment.
  • RBIF — Research Background Inquiry Form: where we provided the detailed research background and information that would later help Chen build the substantive arguments of the case.
  • Publication Record: where we organized the publications we wanted to claim in the petition and provided their bibliographic information by publication type.
  • Recommenders: where potential support-letter signers could be considered after Chen had enough information about the proposed endeavor and research background.

We did not complete all of these in a single sitting, and they were not equally simple. Some required us to think carefully about how to describe technical research in a way that was accurate while still being understandable outside our immediate research community.

These sections were really where the petition began to become our case, rather than simply a collection of personal information and forms.

That is why I am going to go through them separately in the next few parts of this journey — starting with the proposed endeavor.

8. Defining Our Proposed Endeavor

The Proposed Employment/Proposed Endeavor section was especially important because it became the foundation for how our future work would be presented in the NIW petition. We had to describe the research direction my husband intended to continue pursuing in the United States, the kinds of problems he wanted to work on, and the type of research career he expected to follow. Chen then reviewed what we submitted and helped shape it into a clearer immigration narrative.

Once they had enough information, Chen prepared the first draft of our Proposed Endeavor Statement, or PES. The document was intentionally short — only a few pages — and focused on what my husband planned to continue doing, how he intended to advance that work, and eventually why that work was important to the United States. The PES was prepared in two stages: the initial version focused mainly on the proposed endeavor and future employment direction, while the national-importance discussion and supporting evidence were added later so that everything would remain consistent with the final petition letter.

One thing we quickly learned from this stage was that working with an attorney did not mean we could simply hand over our information and wait for a finished document. Chen's team understood the immigration side of the case — how a proposed endeavor should be framed, what USCIS would be looking for, and how the different pieces of the petition needed to fit together. But at the same time, no attorney could understand the technical details of my husband's research as deeply as he did himself.

That became especially clear when we reviewed the first draft of the PES. The overall structure made sense, but some of the wording initially felt a little broad or generic to us, and certain technical descriptions did not fully capture what his research was actually about. That was when we realized how important it was to read every draft carefully rather than assuming that everything was ready simply because it had been prepared by an attorney.

From there, the process became much more collaborative. We asked questions, discussed wording with the legal team, clarified technical concepts, and suggested revisions whenever something did not accurately reflect his work. The attorneys knew how to present the case from an immigration perspective, while we knew the research itself best. Bringing those two sides together was what gradually made the documents feel much more accurate and truly representative of his work.

Once we were comfortable with the preliminary PES, we confirmed it through the portal. That gave Chen the foundation they needed to move forward with the next parts of the case — the support letters and petition letter — while the PES itself would continue to be refined later as the rest of the petition came together.

10. Preparing Our Support Letters

Support letters were an optional part of Chen's service, so this step only applied because we chose a preparation package that included them. At this stage, we provided information about people we thought might be suitable signers through the relevant sections of the portal. We included their professional background, how they knew my husband, and the research or future work they would be able to speak about.

One thing Chen specifically advised us not to do was contact every potential signer right away. Their team first reviewed the information we provided and helped determine which people would be the best fit for the petition. They considered factors such as the person's professional background, familiarity with my husband's work, relationship to him, and the research or proposed endeavor they could meaningfully discuss. Only after the potential signers had been reviewed and selected did we begin reaching out to ask whether they would be willing to sign.

We also learned during this stage that Chen did not treat all support letters the same way. They distinguished between testimonial letters and more traditional recommendation letters. Based on the NIW adjudication trends their team was observing at the time, they preferred testimonial letters for our case because those letters could focus more directly on the proposed endeavor, ongoing or future research, and its broader importance to the United States. Recommendation letters, by comparison, were generally used more to discuss past research achievements and accomplishments.

We originally chose the four-support-letter option because Chen recommended using four letters for our case. Their initial strategy consisted mainly of testimonial letters covering different ongoing or future research projects, together with a recommendation letter that could speak to my husband's past research accomplishments.

After talking it over again between ourselves, however, we decided to reduce the package from four letters to two. This was our own strategic decision rather than something Chen recommended. At the time, we felt that the four people we had identified were probably the strongest potential signers we could realistically approach. We were concerned that if we used all four at the initial filing stage and later received an RFE where additional strong letters might be helpful, we would have fewer comparable options left. For that reason, we preferred to begin with two strong letters while keeping some flexibility in reserve.

Chen agreed to change our preparation package accordingly. The two letters we ultimately submitted were both testimonial letters tied to my husband's ongoing and future research. One was prepared for Dr. X as a National Importance + Ongoing/Future Research Project A testimonial letter, while the other was prepared for Dr. Y as a National Importance + Ongoing/Future Research Project B testimonial letter.

Before Chen could move forward with drafting the letters, we first had to reach out to the selected signers and confirm that they were willing to support the petition. We kept those emails fairly simple: briefly explain that we were preparing an EB-2 NIW permanent-residency petition, explain why that person's perspective would be valuable, ask whether they would be comfortable providing a support letter, and make clear that a draft would be prepared for their review so that the process would require as little of their time as possible. We also made it clear that they could revise anything they felt was necessary before signing.

Once the selected signers agreed, Chen prepared the initial drafts. Just like with the PES, we still reviewed each letter carefully rather than simply accepting the first version. The legal team understood how the letters needed to support the immigration case, but we were still the ones most familiar with my husband's research, the nature of each professional relationship, and the technical details being described. Whenever something felt too general, inaccurate, or did not fully reflect the actual research relationship, we discussed it with Chen and requested revisions.

After we were satisfied with the final wording, we sent each letter to the appropriate signer for their own review, signature, and date. Once the signed versions were returned to us, we uploaded them back to the portal for Chen to include in the petition package.
